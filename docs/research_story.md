# Research Story

This document records how the scientific question changed as hypotheses were falsified. The purpose is to preserve the actual development of the project rather than present a retrospective straight-line success story.

## Stage 1 — Local estimation under nonstationarity

The original problem was practical: a local Koopman/EDMD estimator needs a history length. A short history produces high statistical error, while a long history mixes data generated by different local dynamics.

A first analytic model gave a leading upper-bound structure of the form

\[
U(n,t)\approx \frac{A}{n}+C L_t^2 n^2,
\]

with an upper-bound minimizer scaling as

\[
n^*_{UB}\sim L^{-2/3}.
\]

Numerical checks supported the expected `n^-1` statistical term, `n^2` drift term, and an empirical exponent near `-0.6675` for the minimizer of the derived upper bound.

This was useful as a bias–variance sanity result, but it did not solve causal scale selection because the local drift rate is unknown.

## Stage 2 — Causal nested-window selection

A Lepski-style idea compared estimates from nested histories. The hope was to choose the largest history whose estimates remained statistically compatible.

This failed in the tested setup: valid finite-sample confidence radii were too conservative, many candidate windows were mutually compatible, and the selector often chose the largest history.

The important lesson was not merely that one selector failed. It showed a gap between mathematically valid finite-sample bounds and practically informative online scale selection.

## Stage 3 — Slow spectral structure instead of raw operator error

The project then narrowed the target. Instead of choosing a window that minimizes generic operator error, it asked whether a history length preserves the slow spectral structure most relevant to metastability and long-timescale dynamics.

This motivated comparisons of slow eigenspaces rather than raw operator matrices.

A key sanity test showed why individual eigenvectors were inadequate: after a mode swap, Grassmann distance between the corresponding invariant subspaces was effectively zero while individual-vector overlap could become very small.

From this point onward, invariant subspaces and spectral clusters became the preferred objects whenever possible.

## Stage 4 — The adaptive-window claim weakens

Broad benchmarks used rotating VAR systems, metastable Markov chains, double-well dynamics, AR controls, smooth drift, sharp regime changes, and noise controls. Methods included fixed windows, prediction-loss selection, Lepski-like rules, forgetting, gap criteria, Grassmann criteria, and related online baselines.

The adaptive methods did not consistently dominate simple fixed histories. Some were conservative, some unstable, and some performed well only in selected regimes.

At the same time, literature comparison showed that generic adaptive-window selection, nested-estimator comparison, online operator estimation, and slow-mode tracking all have substantial prior art.

This led to the first major reframing:

> The central claim should not be a generic new adaptive-window method.

Adaptive history selection became a downstream application rather than the scientific core.

## Stage 5 — Nested spectral disagreement is not true drift

The project had used

\[
S_{scale}(H,t)=d_G(E_H(t),E_{2H}(t))
\]

as an intuitive measure of whether older history remained dynamically compatible.

A decisive controlled test compared this observable with known true dynamical drift. The correlation was poor. Nested disagreement behaved primarily as scale-dependent estimator inconsistency rather than a clean drift signal.

This falsified the original direct "coherence horizon" interpretation.

The project pivoted again:

> Instead of treating disagreement as drift, study whether it is a useful **spectral reliability signal**.

## Stage 6 — Reliability in regular-gap systems

E8 tested cross-scale reliability in a stationary, regular-gap linear-EDMD family. Here the signal was genuinely informative about spectral estimation error, with moderate correlation, AUROC near `0.79`, and useful calibration gains.

E9 moved from individual modes toward clusters. E9b fixed the outer spectral gap and tested a two-dimensional invariant cluster. In the hardest primary condition, cluster reliability reached AUROC around `0.83`, support near `1`, and substantially improved quantitative calibration.

This was the first strong positive result of the reliability framing.

However, the claim was deliberately restricted to the tested stationary regular-gap family.

## Stage 7 — Transport failure under non-normality

E10 asked whether the normal-regime reliability calibration transported to non-normal systems with controlled eigenvalues and gaps.

It did not.

At high non-normality, the intended `r=2` spectral object became undefined much more often. Operator-level error diagnostics transported better than the spectral/subspace calibration.

This suggested that non-normality was changing the type of failure rather than merely amplifying the same error.

## Stage 8 — Object existence becomes the scientific target

E11 used matched finite operator perturbations to isolate the mechanism.

The result was counterintuitive but important:

- non-normality strongly reduced the probability that the selected real/conjugation-closed spectral object remained defined;
- among the objects that survived, conditional Grassmann error did not increase and often decreased.

The main failure mode was perturbation-induced complexification crossing the retained/excluded cluster boundary.

The project therefore distinguished two outcomes:

1. **survivor accuracy** — how accurate the subspace is when it exists;
2. **spectral admissibility** — whether the intended object exists at all.

This distinction became central to the later work.

## Stage 9 — Search for a transportable risk variable

E12 tested whether a first-order-style quantity such as

\[
\kappa\epsilon/g
\]

could provide a transportable calibrated failure law.

It ranked failure fairly well but failed the frozen calibration criterion. In addition, an audit established that one reported separation variable was a raw eigenvalue gap rather than the intended Schur separation, so any stronger claim involving true separation was withdrawn.

The result was retained as evidence that conditioning matters, but not as a universal law.

## Stage 10 — Data-only prediction and a design-limited negative

E13 asked whether summaries from causal nested estimates could predict whether an independent same-budget validation estimate would lose the `r=2` object.

The preregistered prediction target turned out to be too ambitious by construction. Conditional on the underlying operator condition, probe and validation realization noise were independent. A condition-level oracle itself could not reach the success thresholds.

The correct interpretation was therefore **design-limited negative**, not a complete refutation of causal latent-risk prediction.

E13a reanalyzed the same data at the condition-propensity level. Estimated eigenvector conditioning, particularly `kappa_2H`, showed a strong exploratory association with latent failure probability after aggregation.

This result was useful but explicitly post-hoc.

## Stage 11 — Global conditioning is falsified as sufficient

E14a designed matched operator families to test whether global eigenvector conditioning was actually the governing risk variable.

It was not.

At matched eigenvalues, spectral gaps, perturbation norm/distribution, and matched global conditioning, moving non-normal coupling from within the retained cluster to the `2|3` retained/excluded boundary produced substantially different pair-cut failure probabilities.

This was a decisive mechanism result:

> spectral admissibility depends on **where the non-normal geometry sits relative to the selected spectral boundary**, not only on a global conditioning scalar.

## Stage 12 — Boundary-specific candidates and a fresh falsification

A mechanism audit of E14a compared a small theory-motivated set of boundary-specific quantities. After correcting an implementation error in the candidate-selection logic, a fixed single-point boundary-resolvent norm was nominated for a fresh test.

E15 froze

\[
R_b=\|(0.55I-A)^{-1}\|_2
\]

and moved to a new four-dimensional operator family.

One primary-panel orientation bug was later corrected without changing the frozen operator pairs or outcomes. The corrected interpretation was sharper than the original automated report:

- when nuisance geometry was closely matched, the larger resolvent had the predicted higher risk;
- but when the fixed resolvent was tightly matched and other geometry changed, admissibility risk could differ dramatically.

Therefore E15 did **not** show that resolvent information is useless. It showed that a single scalar resolvent value is **not sufficient as a transportable risk variable**.

E15-R then examined local resolvent shape, singular geometry, modal coupling, and a projected boundary discriminant. The projected `2x2` discriminant explained much of the high-risk structural type, but the matched-resolvent counterexample reduced to too few independent structural families to identify one unique replacement mechanism.

The project stopped short of promoting another post-hoc scalar.

## Stage 13 — Returning to causal source attribution

A parallel branch returned to the earlier reliability question: even if spectral error is predictable, can the source of observed instability be identified as finite-sample estimation error versus true smooth drift?

The first EXP-009 Gate-1 pilot failed because the two regimes had insufficient overlap in total observable instability magnitude. Rather than treating this as a scientific result, the design was recalibrated with fresh pilot seeds until same-history matched overlap was achieved.

The frozen confirmatory test then used fresh trajectories and fixed development/test condition pairs.

The result was a strong negative:

- individual scale, temporal, operator-change, and gap diagnostics were near chance for source classification;
- joint diagnostics did not improve on them;
- the matched-overlap design ensured the result was not simply caused by a trivial difference in total instability amplitude.

At the same time, cross-scale disagreement still correlated with the actual slow-subspace estimation error.

This established a new conceptual separation:

> **Reliability may be predictable without the source of unreliability being identifiable.**

## Present position

The project has moved far from its initial adaptive-window formulation.

The current scientific picture is built around three linked problems:

1. **Reliability:** can causal observables predict actual spectral estimation error?
2. **Admissibility:** when does the intended slow spectral object cease to exist under finite-data/operator perturbations?
3. **Identifiability:** can one determine why an observed spectral change occurred, or are estimation noise and true drift observationally confounded?

Adaptive history selection remains a possible application only after these reliability questions are better understood.
